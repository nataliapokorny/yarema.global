title: The Vacuum Trick for Mass-Producing Nanocrystals

excerpt: A clever underpressure technique lets labs scale up nanocrystal
  production up to 200 times — without expensive industrial equipment.

Making perfectly sized nanocrystals is a lot like cooking a delicate recipe where timing is everything. To get particles that are all exactly the same size, scientists use a method called hot-injection. This involves instantly shooting a lot of room-temperature chemical ingredients into a flask of hot liquids. This sudden mixture creates a quick burst of miniscule nanocrystal seeds that all grow uniformly. It works beautifully for tiny, test-tube-sized batches. But there is a major physical problem: how do you inject giant volumes of liquid instantly if you want to make an industrial-sized batch? You simply cannot push a giant syringe fast enough by hand.



In a recent study, we solved this scaling problem using a clever physics trick. Instead of pushing the liquid into the flask, they pulled it in. By pumping some of the air out of the hot flask before the reaction started, we created a mild vacuum, called underpressure. When we opened the valve to the new chemical ingredients, the underpressure inside the flask acted like a vacuum cleaner. It violently sucked in massive amounts of liquid — up to 150 milliliters per second! This allowed us to mix huge volumes in just two or three seconds.



To prove that the underpressure method worked, we scaled up recipes for four different types of nanocrystals used in modern solar cells, LEDs, and batteries. The experiment was a massive success. We could increased our production by up to 200 times, creating tens of grams of nanocrystals at once instead of a few milligrams. Most importantly, the large-batch crystals were just as perfectly shaped and highly functional as the small-batch ones.



This simple vacuum trick proves that regular laboratories can easily mass-produce nanomaterials without needing highly expensive, custom-built factory equipment.
