Gold nanoparticles are incredibly useful nanoscale tools. Because of how they uniquely interact with light and chemicals, known as plasmon resonance, engineers use them for everything from advanced medical testing to clean energy production. However, gold nanoparticles have a major weakness: when they get hot, they easily melt and clump together, which ruins their special abilities. To fix this, scientists often try to wrap the gold in a protective shell made of oxides. But painting a perfectly smooth and particularly ultrathin oxide coating is an extreme manufacturing challenge.


In a recent study, we discovered a brilliant workaround. Rather than trying to coat the outside of the nanoparticle, we made them create an oxide skin from the inside out!


Instead of starting with pure gold, we created a mixed metal alloy of gold and galium. When we placed these gold-gallium nanoalloy particles into a mild chemical bath, it triggered a fascinating reaction. The gallium atoms migrated to the outside surface of the particle. Once they hit the outside edge, they oxidized – turning into an ultrathin crust of gallium oxide. This left a pure gold core behind that is perfectly sealed.


We found that we can control exactly how thick the shell became simply by changing the amount of gallium in the original mix. We also monitored the entire process using light. Because the mixed gold-gallium alloy and the pure gold reflect light differently, we can track the color shifts to measure and see the nanoarmor growing in real time without needing a microscope.


When we tested our new invention, the results were highly encouraging. This sub-nanometer suit of armor successfully prevented the gold nanoparticles from melting and clumping, even when baked at 150 °C for the whole day. Our inside-out coating method gives engineers a powerful new way to build tough, long-lasting nanomaterials for the technologies of tomorrow, where concentrated and intense light is in use.
